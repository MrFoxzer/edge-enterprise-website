What a Framing Warranty Actually Covers

Published July 7, 2026 · 6 min read · By the Edge Enterprise crew

A framing warranty covers one thing: workmanship. It's the framer's written promise that the structure was built to the plans, the specs, and an agreed tolerance — and that if something wasn't, they come back and make it right at their own cost for a defined term, typically one year from substantial completion on commercial work. What it doesn't cover matters just as much: normal wood movement, other trades' damage, and problems that trace back to the drawings instead of the crew. Here's how to read one before you sign.

The three layers of coverage

When people say "framing warranty," they're usually mixing three separate things:

  1. The workmanship warranty. The framer's own promise that the work matches the contract documents. This is the piece you actually negotiate, and the piece this post is about.
  2. Material and manufacturer warranties. Trusses, engineered lumber, hangers, and connectors carry their manufacturers' warranties, which pass through to the owner. If an LVL delaminates, that claim runs to the manufacturer — the framer's role is documentation and sorting out who covers replacement labor.
  3. Statutory warranties. Minnesota puts mandatory warranties on residential new construction that a contract can't waive. Commercial work is different: there, the warranty is whatever the subcontract says — which is exactly why the language deserves a read before buyout.

What workmanship coverage actually gets you

On our jobs, the workmanship warranty means:

  • Walls plumb, level, and square within the stated tolerance
  • Layout per the architectural plans — rooms, partitions, and rough openings where the drawings put them
  • Connections, hold-downs, and hardware installed per the structural drawings and hardware schedule
  • If an inspector, the GC's punch walk, or a downstream trade turns up framing that doesn't meet that standard, we correct it — including the callback after drywall if it surfaces late

What it doesn't cover — and why

Wood moves. Dimensional lumber goes into the wall at up to 19 percent moisture content and dries down over the building's first year, and a platform-framed floor can lose 3/8" or more of height per level as plates and joists shrink. That drying is what causes most first-year nail pops, hairline drywall cracks at corners and headers, and the occasional squeak — and no framer warranties it, because it isn't a defect. It's physics. The buildings we framed over the winter are going through exactly that drying cycle this summer.

Also outside the warranty:

  • Other trades' damage — over-notched studs, cut truss chords, joists drilled outside the allowed zone. We flag these when we see them, but the fix bills to whoever did the cutting.
  • Weather exposure — how long the frame stands open before roofing and wrap is a schedule call made above the framer. Swelling and staining from months of exposure isn't a workmanship issue.
  • Design deficiencies — if we built the detail that's on the drawings and the detail is the problem, that's a design conversation, not a warranty claim.

Tolerances: the number that settles arguments

Most warranty disputes are really tolerance disputes. The common reference for rough framing is 1/4" out of plumb or level over 10 feet — that's the standard we hold our crews to and check with lasers before we call a floor ready for the next trade. Whatever number you use, put it in the subcontract. "The wall looks wavy" is an argument; "the wall is 5/8" out in 8 feet" is a punch item with a clear owner.

What to ask a framing sub before buyout

  • Is the warranty term and start date in writing — and does it run from substantial completion or from the framer's demobilization?
  • What tolerance standard is named?
  • What's the committed response time on a callback?
  • If a correction needs an engineer's fix letter, who pays for it?

A sub who answers those four questions without flinching is a sub who plans to still be around in year one. We've framed more than 1,000 units across Minnesota, North Dakota, Wisconsin, and Montana on those terms — projects like the Rogers senior living build — and the callback list stays short when the frame gets checked before drywall, not after.
