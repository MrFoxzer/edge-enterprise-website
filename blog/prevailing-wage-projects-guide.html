Working Prevailing-Wage Projects: A Sub's Guide to Doing It Right

Published June 2, 2026 · 4 min read · By the Edge Enterprise crew

Prevailing-wage work scares off a lot of framing subs, and usually for the wrong reason. The wages themselves aren't the trap — a good framer in this market earns close to the prevailing rate anyway. What actually sinks subs on public work is everything around the paycheck: wage decisions nobody read at bid time, workers classified wrong, and certified payroll that shows up late, sloppy, or not at all. We've framed publicly funded projects across Minnesota and North Dakota, and the difference between a smooth job and a compliance mess comes down to habits you build before the first wall goes up.

Figure out which rules apply before you price anything

Prevailing wage follows the money, not the building. Federal dollars — HUD-financed multi-family is the big one in our world — generally mean Davis-Bacon rates and federal certified payroll. State bonding money, county buildings, school districts, and a lot of affordable-housing financing trigger state prevailing wage instead, and Minnesota's rates and rules aren't the same as North Dakota's. Sometimes both apply and the higher rate wins. Treat the wage decision as a bid document, same as the plans and specs. If it isn't in the bid package, ask the GC for it in writing before you submit a number.

Read the wage decision like you read the plans

Rates vary by county and by classification, and the number that matters is the total package: base rate plus fringe. You owe the whole thing — either as cash on the check or as contributions to bona fide benefit plans. Check whether the project falls under commercial or residential rate schedules, too; on wood-frame multi-family that distinction can move your labor line significantly, and guessing wrong hurts in either direction. Bid it wrong and you're either leaving money on the table or funding the difference out of your margin for the entire job. Our commercial framing cost guide covers how labor drives framing numbers in general; on prevailing-wage work, that labor line just gets a lot less forgiving.

Classification is where framing subs get burned

Not everyone on a framing crew is a carpenter for payroll purposes, and not everyone is a laborer either. The framer cutting and setting walls is doing carpenter work. The hand packing material and cleaning up may be a laborer. The problem is the guy who does both — which on a real framing crew is almost everybody. Two rules keep us out of trouble. First, track split classifications with actual timekeeping, not end-of-week memory. Second, when in doubt, classify up. The few dollars an hour saved by classifying down is nothing next to back wages, penalties, and a GC who has to answer for your payroll and never calls you again.

Certified payroll is a weekly habit, not a closeout scramble

Certified payroll is due weekly — the federal WH-347 or the state's format — and it has to match reality: names, classifications, hours, rates, fringes, deductions. Somebody in your office needs to own it as a standing Friday task, the same way somebody owns payroll itself. Keep the records for at least three years, and expect spot checks: agencies do jobsite interviews, and what your crew says about their work and their pay needs to line up with what you filed. If your bookkeeping can't support that today, fix the bookkeeping before you chase public work — not after you've won it.

Pay the fringe or prove the fringe

The fringe portion of the rate isn't optional and it isn't vague. Either it goes on the check as cash, or it goes into bona fide plans — health insurance, retirement, apprenticeship funds — and you can document exactly what was contributed per hour, per worker. Loose promises about benefits don't count. This is the single most common gap we see when subs new to public work get audited.

If you're a GC: vet the paperwork, not just the price

In Minnesota, a sub's wage violations can land on the contractor above them, so a framing bid that only pencils at private-market labor rates is a liability, not a bargain. Ask a prospective sub for a sample certified payroll from a past public job, ask who prepares it, and ask how they handle split classifications. We put that in front of GCs up front — our for-GCs page covers how we run compliance, insurance, and documentation on every project — because a sub who's organized on paper is usually organized on the deck too.
