The Drywall-Ready Checklist: How Good Framers Hand Off a Building

Published March 31, 2026 · 4 min read · By the Edge Enterprise crew

The best measure of a framing crew isn't how fast the walls went up — it's what the drywall foreman says on his first walk through the building. A clean handoff means rockers hang without stopping, tapers aren't fighting wavy walls, and the schedule holds. A sloppy one means back-charges, blame, and two trades pointing fingers while the job bleeds days. Here's the checklist our foremen run before we ever call a building drywall-ready.

What "drywall-ready" actually means

It's more than "the framing is done." Drywall-ready means every surface that receives rock is straight, solid, and backed; everything that has to happen inside the walls is finished and inspected; and nothing about the framing will surprise the next trade. On a turnkey scope, this handoff is our responsibility from start to finish — we covered what that includes in our post on turnkey framing.

Walls, ceilings, and the straightness test

  • Plumb and straight: Walls string-lined, badly crowned studs culled or planed. The standard we hold ourselves to: if you can see the wave from across the room, the taper will see it in every raking light for the life of the building.
  • Ceiling nailers: Backing or drywall clips at every ceiling-to-wall intersection and every parallel-to-joist condition. Missing ceiling nailers are the single most common item on a drywall foreman's gripe list.
  • Corners: Three-stud or California corners with a nailing surface on both faces — no dead corners where the rock has nothing to grab.

Backing, blocking, and openings

  • Backing complete: Cabinets, closet shelving, toilet accessories, handrails, TV locations, door stops — installed per the backing plan, not from memory.
  • Rough openings verified: Every window and door RO checked against the schedule, square and to size, with sills and kings intact where other trades cut through.
  • Repairs made: Studs the plumbers and electricians notched or over-bored are sistered or repaired per code, not left for the inspector to catch.

Inside the walls

  • Fire blocking and draft stopping complete at every required location — soffits, chases, stair stringers, double walls.
  • Structural sign-offs closed: Shear nailing, hold-downs, straps, and hardware inspected and approved. Rock hung over an uninspected shear wall comes back down, and that mistake belongs to whoever hung it — don't put your drywaller in that position.
  • Soffits and drops built: Every mechanical soffit, dropped ceiling, and chase framed now — not "we'll come back for it." Later never comes cheap.

The handoff walk

Checklists on paper only go so far. Before demobilizing a floor or a building, our foreman walks it with the GC's super and, whenever the schedule allows, the drywall foreman — the actual customer for this work. Blue tape goes on anything questionable, the punch gets worked the same week, and the walk gets repeated until it's clean. Ten minutes of a drywall foreman pointing at things saves both trades a week of change-order paperwork.

Why this is a schedule issue, not just a quality issue

Fixing framing before rock costs a carpenter and an hour. Fixing it after rock costs demo, rehang, retape, retexture, and repaint — call it several times the cost, plus the argument over who pays. On a multi-family job turning over floors in sequence, one bad handoff repeats itself twenty times. The GCs we work with year after year tell us the same thing: they can find framers who frame fast, but framers who finish clean are the ones who get the next building. That finish-clean standard is baked into every scope on our services page, whether it's a garden-style walkup or a five-story podium job.

With framing season opening up across Minnesota, it's a fair question to put to every framing bid on your desk: ask what their drywall-ready checklist looks like. If the answer is a shrug, the low number isn't actually low.
