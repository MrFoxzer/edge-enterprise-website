Planning Spring Builds in January: The Head Start Most Owners Miss

Published December 9, 2025 · 3 min read · By the Edge Enterprise crew

Spring feels a long way off from the second week of December, but here is what we watch play out every single year: the owners and GCs who break ground in April made their key decisions in January, and the ones who start making calls when the snow melts end up framing in July — or waiting until next year. Pre-construction has a longer tail than most owners think, and winter is when that tail either gets handled or gets ignored.

Work backward from the day you want walls going up

Say you want framing to start the first week of May. Walk it backward:

  • Foundations need to be poured, cured, and backfilled ahead of us — which realistically means breaking ground in early April, weather permitting.
  • Permits for commercial and multi-family work commonly take four to twelve weeks in review, depending on the jurisdiction and how clean the submittal is.
  • Truss and floor system shop drawings take time to produce, review, and fabricate. In the spring rush, truss lead times stretch to two or three months. In January, they are a fraction of that.
  • Framing bids and buyout take two to four weeks when the drawings are ready — longer when every trade in the region is pricing the same spring backlog.

Stack those up and a May framing start traces straight back to decisions made in January. Miss January and the whole chain slides — into the busiest, most expensive season of the year.

What a January start actually buys you

Better pricing, held longer

Lumber and truss pricing has historically been softer in winter than in the spring run-up, and suppliers are far more willing to hold a quote for a project buying in their slow season. The same goes for labor: a project that shows up in April asking for a May start pays rush rates, because the calendar is already full. If you want to understand where the money in a framing package actually goes before you start those conversations, our commercial framing cost guide breaks it down line by line.

First pick of crew slots

Our spring calendar fills in late winter, every year. January conversations get first pick of start dates; April conversations get whatever is left. That is not a sales line — it is how every established framing contractor in the Upper Midwest runs, and it is the difference between framing in May and framing in August.

Time for the decisions that are expensive to rush

Wood versus light-gauge steel, panelized versus stick-built, floor system selection, turnkey versus labor-only — each of those changes cost, schedule, and the drawings themselves. Made in January, they are design decisions. Made in April, they are change orders. If you are still weighing structure type, start with our comparison of wood versus steel framing for commercial projects.

The January checklist

  1. Get drawings to a permit-ready state and submit early — review queues only get longer from February on.
  2. Send plans out for framing bids, even at 80 percent design. A real number now beats a perfect number in April, and you can run a quick sanity check first with our ballpark estimate calculator.
  3. Have your framer start pricing the truss and lumber package so quotes can be locked before spring movement.
  4. Reserve a crew window with a realistic start date and a named backup date.
  5. Walk site logistics while the site is quiet — staging areas, crane access, delivery routes.

None of that requires finished construction documents. It requires a set of plans that is close, and an owner willing to spend the winter working instead of waiting.
