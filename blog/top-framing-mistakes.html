The 7 Most Expensive Framing Mistakes We See (and How to Avoid Them)

Published May 19, 2026 · 5 min read · By the Edge Enterprise crew

After framing more than 1,000 units of multi-family and commercial work across Minnesota, North Dakota, Wisconsin, and Montana, we keep seeing the same handful of expensive mistakes — on other people's jobs, and early in our own history, on a few of ours. Here are the seven that cost the most, roughly in the order they show up on a project.

1. Taking the foundation on faith

Setting sill plates without shooting the foundation first is the original sin of framing. Concrete that is out of level or out of square doesn't stay a concrete problem — it telegraphs into every floor above it, and by the third level you are fighting drywall cracks, cabinet reveals, and doors that won't hang square. The fix: survey every foundation before the first plate, document what you find, and get the tolerance conversation over with on day one.

2. Layout mistakes that compound

A bearing wall set a half-inch off on the first floor becomes an inch by the third and a plumbing chase that doesn't stack by the fourth. Layout is the highest-leverage hour on a framing job, and it is routinely handed to whoever is available instead of whoever is best. The fix: your most experienced people run layout, a second set of eyes checks critical dimensions, and unit-to-unit dimensions get verified as floors stack — not assumed.

3. Missing or substituted structural hardware

Hangers, hold-downs, straps, and clips are the least glamorous items on the structural sheets and the most commonly botched — missing nails, wrong fasteners, or a "close enough" substitution from the truck. Inspectors catch some of it. The rest waits for a windstorm. The fix: a hardware takeoff at bid time, hardware installed as framing progresses rather than in a cleanup pass, and a dedicated hardware walk before every inspection.

4. Ignoring shrinkage in multi-story wood framing

Wood loses moisture after it's framed, and plates and joists shrink across the grain — stack four floors and the building can settle enough to crack finishes, stress plumbing risers, and open gaps at exterior trim if nobody planned for it. The fix: detail for movement where the structural drawings call for it, keep material dry on site, and sequence finishes with the framing schedule so the building has time to give up its moisture.

5. No blocking and backing plan

Cabinets, grab bars, handrails, and TV mounts all need solid material behind the drywall, and "the crew knows to block for that" is not a plan. Every missed location becomes a cut-patch-paint repair during finishes, at the worst possible point in the schedule. The fix: a written backing plan pulled from the architectural set and shop drawings, standard heights posted in every unit, and photos of every wall before cover.

6. Framing through unanswered questions

The set says one thing, the field says another, and the crew builds something plausible to keep moving. Sometimes it matches what the engineer wanted. When it doesn't, the rework costs ten times what the RFI would have. The fix: batch the questions in precon, and when a new conflict surfaces mid-build, stop that wall — not the job — until there's an answer in writing.

7. Skipping the pre-drywall walk

The last chance to fix anything cheaply is the week before insulation. Bowed studs, missed blocking, hardware gaps, out-of-plumb walls — all of it is a fifteen-minute fix with the framing exposed and a change order once it's covered. The fix: a formal framing punch, walked by the framer and the GC together, signed off before insulation is scheduled.

The pattern behind all seven

None of these are skill problems. Plenty of talented crews make every one of them, especially in a spring like this one when everyone is racing the calendar. They are system problems — and the fix is always the same: front-load the thinking, write things down, and verify before you cover.
