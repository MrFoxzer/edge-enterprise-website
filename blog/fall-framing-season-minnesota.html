Fall Framing Season in Minnesota: Why September Starts Beat Spring Ones

Published September 9, 2025 · 4 min read · By the Edge Enterprise crew

Given the choice, we'd rather start a multi-family frame in September than in April, and it isn't close. That surprises developers who assume framing season ends when the leaves drop. In Minnesota it's nearly the opposite: a fall start puts you on firm ground, ahead of the spring rush, and dried in with interior trades working all winter — while the spring-start project down the road is still waiting for the site to firm up.

Firm Ground Beats Spring Mud

September and October give you the driest, most stable site conditions of the year. Forklifts move lumber packages without rutting the site, boom lifts set trusses without mats, and deliveries actually reach the building. Compare that to the spring thaw, when a site that was frozen solid in February turns to soup in April. We've watched spring jobs lose more days to towing equipment and rebuilding access roads than to any snowstorm all winter.

Spring Road Restrictions Are Real Money

Every spring, Minnesota counties post seasonal load limits on secondary roads — typically for six to eight weeks starting with the March thaw. If your site sits off a posted road, lumber packages, truss deliveries, and concrete trucks all have to run lighter, which means more trips, split loads, and suppliers charging accordingly. A September start never fights that battle. Your heaviest deliveries — trusses, wall sheathing, floor packages — roll in on frozen or dry roads at full legal weight.

Dried In by the Holidays

A September frame start on a typical three-story walk-up or garden-style building means shell up, roofed, and wrapped before the deep cold sets in. From there, the building becomes its own shelter: mechanical rough-ins, drywall, and finishes run all winter with temporary heat, and you're delivering units into the spring lease-up season — the best time of year to fill a building. Flip it around: a spring frame start delivers into winter, when nobody wants to move. The sequencing behind that is laid out in our multi-family framing schedule guide.

Crews Are Actually Available

Spring is when every GC in the state calls every framing sub in the state, usually the same week. By early fall the calendars loosen up — projects that started on time are topping out, and good crews are looking for the building that carries them through winter. That shows up in responsiveness, in pricing, and in which lead they put on your job.

The Honest Trade-Offs

  • Daylight shrinks. By November we're framing inside a shorter window, so production planning matters more. We front-load the big structural pushes into September and October.
  • Late concrete needs protection. If foundations aren't poured by mid-to-late October, cold-weather concrete measures — blankets, heat, additives — start adding cost. The fix is simple: pour in early fall, frame behind it immediately.
  • Snow is a housekeeping cost, not a stoppage. Sweeping decks and clearing plates takes real crew hours, but framing lumber doesn't care about cold, and neither do crews that are dressed and staffed for it. We frame through Minnesota winters every year.

The short version: fall starts trade a few short days in December for firm ground, full loads, available crews, and a spring delivery. That trade wins almost every time.
