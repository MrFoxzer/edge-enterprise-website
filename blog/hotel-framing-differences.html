Hotel Framing: What's Different From Apartments

Published April 21, 2026 · 5 min read · By the Edge Enterprise crew

On paper, a hotel is an apartment building with smaller units and more of them. On the jobsite, that comparison falls apart fast. We frame both, and the crews that move between them will tell you a hotel punishes sloppiness in ways an apartment building forgives. Here's what actually changes when the plans say flag prototype instead of multi-family.

The prototype drives everything — including your tolerances

Most branded hotels are built from a national prototype adapted to the site. That means the guest room has been engineered down to the inch: the casework, the headboard blocking, the tub or shower unit, sometimes an entire prefabricated bathroom pod, all arrive sized for a room that's assumed to be exactly what the drawings say. In an apartment, a demising wall that drifts half an inch gets absorbed by drywall and trim. In a hotel guest room, it means the vanity doesn't fit — and a hundred identical rooms have the same problem. We hold layout tighter on hotels: control lines checked every floor, room dimensions verified before the next deck goes on. Errors don't just repeat here, they compound.

More rated assemblies, more of the time

Hotels carry heavier sound and fire requirements between rooms than most market-rate apartments. Expect more shaft liner, more double-stud and staggered-stud demising walls, resilient channel, and specific assembly details the brand will actually inspect. The framing itself isn't harder — but the sequencing is less forgiving, and a crew that treats a listed assembly like a generic partition creates rework that surfaces at the worst possible time: right before opening.

The first floor is a different building

Guest room floors are repetitive. The first floor is not. Lobbies, breakfast areas, meeting rooms, pools, fitness rooms, laundry, and kitchens all want open spans and tall ceilings under four stories of tightly gridded rooms above. That means transfer framing, big engineered beams, and often a switch in structural system — many hotels in our markets run light-gauge steel or a hybrid where an apartment building would be all wood. The pool area adds its own layer: humidity-driven detailing and treated or engineered material choices that don't exist anywhere else in the building.

Openings, PTAC boxes, and the wall that's mostly holes

Every guest room exterior wall carries a window and usually a PTAC or VTAC opening, framed to the manufacturer's rough opening at a repeatable height, hundreds of times over. Add tub boxes, chase walls, and connecting-room door pairs, and hotel walls carry far more coordination detail per lineal foot than apartment walls do. We build these repetitively — templates, story poles, pre-cut opening packages — because field-measuring the same opening 120 times is how mistakes creep in.

The schedule has a hard stop

Apartments lease up unit by unit; a delayed building mostly costs carry. A hotel has a flag opening date with reservations, staff hiring, and brand commitments stacked behind it, and everything upstream of that date gets compressed — starting with framing, since nothing dries in until we're done. The floor-by-floor rhythm looks like multi-family sequencing, but the float is thinner and the finish trades stack in behind you faster. A framer who's honest about durations matters more here than almost anywhere else.

What to look for in a hotel framer

  • Experience with brand prototypes and their inspection regimes, not just generic commercial work
  • Layout discipline — ask how they control dimensions floor to floor
  • Comfort in both wood and light-gauge steel, since many hotels use both in the same building
  • A crew size that can actually hold the floor-cycle pace the schedule assumes

We frame hotels and apartments across MN, ND, WI, and MT — wood and light-gauge steel, turnkey or labor-only — and we bid them knowing the difference.
