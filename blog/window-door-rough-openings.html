How Big Should a Window Rough Opening Be? A Framer's Guide

Published September 29, 2026 · 6 min read · By the Edge Enterprise crew

The short answer: build the rough opening to the size the window or door manufacturer lists for that exact unit — not the size on the plan, not the nominal size, and not a guess. For most vinyl and clad-wood windows that works out to roughly 1/2 inch wider and 1/2 inch taller than the unit's outside frame, which leaves about 1/4 inch of shim and sealant space all the way around. Doors run tighter or looser depending on the brand. The number that governs is the one on the manufacturer's spec sheet for the product that's actually getting delivered.

Why the plan number isn't enough

Architectural window schedules usually call out a unit by its nominal or catalog size — a "3050" or "3046" — and sometimes a rough opening that was pulled from a different product line than what got bought. Then value engineering happens, the GC switches brands to hit a lead time, and nobody updates the schedule. Crews frame what's on paper, and in November the windows don't fit.

That's why we ask for the approved submittal or the supplier's quote with rough opening dimensions before we start layout on any job with more than a handful of openings. On a multi-family building with a few hundred windows, one wrong assumption gets repeated a few hundred times.

How we frame a rough opening

Every opening in a bearing wall has the same basic parts, and each one affects the final size:

  • King studs run full height on each side and tie the header back into the wall.
  • Jack (trimmer) studs carry the header down to the bottom plate. The inside faces of the jacks set the rough opening width.
  • The header is sized by the engineer or the code span tables for the load above it. Its bottom face sets the top of the rough opening.
  • The rough sill sets the bottom of the opening. Cripples fill in above the header and below the sill to keep the stud layout for sheathing and drywall.

Rough opening height is where most mistakes live. Header height is usually set once for the whole building — commonly so the tops of windows and doors line up — and the sill height gets figured by subtracting the RO height from that line. If someone changes a unit height and doesn't recalculate the sill, you get an opening that's right in width and wrong in height.

Tolerances that actually matter

  • Square. Diagonals within about 1/8 inch of each other on a typical window. Much more and the installer is fighting the frame.
  • Plumb jacks and a level sill. A sill that's out of level puts the whole window out, and shimming a crowned sill is a bad fix. Pick straight stock for sills and jacks.
  • Not too big. An oversized opening sounds harmless, but a 1-inch gap on one side means the installer is stacking shims and relying on foam to fill a void the flashing wasn't designed for.
  • Sloped sills where specified. Some manufacturers and many envelope consultants want a sloped sill or back dam under the sill pan. That changes your framed height slightly — figure it before you cut, not after.

Doors are a different animal

Exterior door rough openings depend on whether the unit comes prehung with a sill, what the finished floor is going to be, and whether the threshold sits on the subfloor or gets recessed. A door opening framed for a 3/4-inch floor that ends up with tile over a mortar bed will have a threshold sitting low or a door that won't clear. Ask the question early: what's the finished floor at every exterior door?

The sheathing and wrap step

Once sheathing goes on, we route openings flush with the framing and leave them clean for the flashing sequence. If the building uses a system sheathing like ZIP, the tape and the sill pan details start right at our rough opening, which is one more reason the framing needs to be square and true. We covered that tradeoff in our post on ZIP System vs. housewrap.

Why this matters more in the fall

Across Minnesota and the Dakotas, the goal this time of year is a building that's dried in before the snow sticks. Windows are usually the last piece of that puzzle, and they're also the piece with the longest lead time. If windows land in November and the openings don't fit, you're re-framing in the cold while the building sits wrapped in poly and burning temp heat.

Our fall checklist for any job with windows arriving before winter:

  1. Get the approved window and door submittal with RO dimensions before layout starts.
  2. Confirm header height and sill height for each unit type, and write them on the walls.
  3. Spot-check the first floor's openings against actual delivered units if any are on site.
  4. Plan temporary protection for openings if the windows are running late — plywood or framed poly covers that won't get torn off in a November wind.
  5. Flag any late product substitutions to the GC in writing before the next floor gets framed.

It just has to happen before the saw comes out, which is the same point we make in our pre-bid checklist for GCs. It's also part of how we coordinate scope with the GCs we frame for.
