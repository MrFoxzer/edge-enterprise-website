Crane Day: Planning the Most Expensive Hours on a Framing Job

Published June 16, 2026 · 4 min read · By the Edge Enterprise crew

On most days, a framing job burns labor one man at a time. On crane day, everything burns at once: the crane, the operator, and your entire crew are on the clock simultaneously, and the meter runs whether trusses are flying or everyone is standing around waiting on a decision. Add a ten-man crew to a machine that costs more per hour than three framers, and an idle hour on crane day becomes the most expensive hour on the whole project. So we plan those hours harder than any others.

Know what the day actually costs

Crane pricing has two parts: mobilization each way — often a four-figure line item before the outriggers ever come down — and an hourly rate with operator that typically runs a few hundred dollars. Stack your whole crew's wages on top of that, because they're all committed to the pick. The point of running this math isn't precision; it's motivation. Once you price an idle crane hour honestly, prepping the day stops feeling optional.

Build a pick plan, not a wish list

Before we book the crane, we know the pick list in order, the heaviest and longest pieces, and the radius to the farthest set — because weight at radius is what tells the crane company which machine to send. When there's any doubt, we walk the setup spot with them: ground conditions and matting, underground utilities, overhead lines, and swing room. One signal person, one voice on the radio, agreed before the first pick leaves the ground.

Sequence the trucks so the crane never waits

The best truss day is one where trusses fly straight off the truck in setting order — no double-handling, no yard full of stacks to dig through. That takes a phone call to the truss plant about banding and load order, and a delivery window coordinated with the crane. Wall panels get staged in reverse order so the first panel needed is on top. If deliveries can't be sequenced, we re-stack the day before, on cheap hours instead of crane hours.

Have the building ready before the crane shows up

Bearing walls plumbed, lined, and braced. Interior bearing blocked. Truss layout marked on the plates. Bracing material pre-cut, bundled, and distributed across the deck. Gable ends sheathed on the ground where the design allows, tag lines tied on, and the first-truss bracing plan agreed the day before. The crane's job is to fly material — everything that can be done at deck level beforehand gets done beforehand.

Make the weather call early — especially in summer

A 60-foot truss is a sail. Most crews shut down picks somewhere around 20 mph of wind, and long spans come out of the sky earlier than that. Minnesota summers hand you calm mornings and gusty, stormy afternoons, so we book cranes for first light and aim to have the risky picks done by early afternoon. We make the go, no-go, or half-day call the night before — that conversation is a lot cheaper before the crane mobilizes than after it's set up on site.

Ground crew, top crew, and nobody under a load

Crane day runs on defined roles: the ground crew rigs, stabilizes, and manages tag lines; the top crew sets and braces; and roles don't switch mid-day. The morning pre-task talk covers pick order, signals, and exclusion zones, and the rule that never bends is that nobody works under a suspended load. A crew that knows exactly who does what moves fast precisely because nobody is improvising.

Crane day is also where the schedule gets won — a well-planned truss day puts a lid on a building that took weeks to frame, which is why it anchors the sequencing in our multi-family framing schedule guide. GCs can see how we coordinate cranes, deliveries, and hoisting scope on our for-GCs page.
