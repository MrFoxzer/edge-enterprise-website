How to Read Framing Plans: A Field Guide

Published August 26, 2025 · 5 min read · By the Edge Enterprise crew

Half of the expensive framing mistakes we've torn out over the years trace back to the same root cause: somebody read the plans wrong — or read them once at the trailer and never looked again. After 1,000-plus units framed across Minnesota, North Dakota, Wisconsin, and Montana, we've trained a lot of crew leads to read a set the way we do. Here's the field version: the order we actually read plans in, and the traps that catch people.

Start With the Structural Sheets, Not the Architectural

The architectural set tells you what the building looks like. The structural set — the S-sheets — tells you what holds it up. Our leads start every job by reading the general structural notes top to bottom. It's the most boring page in the set, and it answers half the questions the crew will ask all job: lumber species and grade, sheathing specs, nailing requirements, allowable fastener substitutions, blocking rules. When a framer asks whether he can sister that joist, the answer is usually already printed on S0.1.

Learn the Schedules Before the Floor Plan

On commercial and multi-family work, the schedules are where the money lives:

  • Shear wall schedule. Sheathing thickness, nail size, edge and field spacing, one side or both. A wall tagged SW-2 is not the same animal as SW-4, even if they look identical once framed — and the inspector will check the nailing on both.
  • Hold-down schedule. Which hardware goes at which wall end and what it anchors into. Miss one before the deck above goes on and you're coring concrete and epoxying anchors later.
  • Header and beam schedule. Sizes, plies, and the line everybody skips: required bearing. If the schedule calls for two jack studs under a header, it gets two. Point loads have to stack all the way to the foundation.

Follow Every Callout to the Actual Detail

A detail bubble like 5/S4.1 means detail 5 on sheet S4.1, and it exists because something at that spot isn't typical. In our experience the callouts cluster exactly where the mistakes cluster: shear transfer at floor lines, beam pockets, cantilevered decks, stair openings, and anywhere wood meets steel or concrete. If a lead can't explain what the detail at a given corner is doing, the crew isn't framing that corner yet.

Know What the Dimensions Are Pulled To

Face of stud, face of sheathing, centerline of wall, face of finish — every architect picks a convention, and the cover sheet or general notes tell you which one. Assume wrong and you're an inch and a half off, repeated down a 200-foot corridor, and the error surfaces months later when the prefab cabinets or the elevator entrance won't fit. Same warning for window openings: pull rough opening sizes from the window schedule, and never scale anything off a printed sheet.

When Sheets Disagree, Stop and Write the RFI

This time of year, everyone is pushing to get buildings dried in before the weather turns, and the pressure to just keep framing is real. Resist it. When the architectural plan puts a wall at one dimension and the structural plan puts a shear wall somewhere else, that's not a judgment call — that's a one-paragraph RFI. A one-day answer beats a one-week tear-out every time. Chasing down those conflicts before they hit the field is a big part of what GCs are paying for in a turnkey framing package.

Read the Drawings That Aren't in the Set

Truss placement diagrams and floor panel layouts come from the manufacturer, not the architect, and they arrive with their own hanger callouts, girder locations, and bearing points. Every truss package also ships with permanent bracing requirements the inspector will want to see followed on site. Treat the truss layout like a plan sheet, because functionally it is one.

A framing sub that reads plans well saves a GC more money than one that just swings hammers fast — fewer surprises, fewer failed inspections, fewer callbacks. It's the standard we hold every lead to, and it's a big part of how we run jobs for general contractors — more on that on our For GCs page.
