Construction Scheduling Through the Holidays Without Losing December

Published November 25, 2025 · 3 min read · By the Edge Enterprise crew

December is the month construction schedules quietly lose, and most of the loss has nothing to do with weather. Between Thanksgiving — this week — and January 2, a typical crew calendar holds three holidays, a stretch of half-staffed days, and two weeks when suppliers, inspectors, and engineers are hard to reach. You cannot fight that. You can plan around it, and the GCs who do come out of the holidays two or three weeks ahead of the ones who treat December like a normal month.

Count the real working days

Look at what December actually gives you this year:

  • Christmas and New Year's both land on a Thursday, which turns two full weeks into two long weekends. Most crews take the Friday after; plenty take the whole week.
  • Daylight bottoms out around nine hours in our part of the Upper Midwest. Crane picks, sheathing, and setting walls all want light, so productive hours shrink even on full days.
  • Suppliers wind down mid-month. Lumberyards and truss plants run skeleton crews between Christmas and New Year's. The last fully reliable delivery window is roughly December 19. Order like it.
  • Inspectors and engineers take time off too. A rough-in inspection or an RFI answer that has to happen between the holidays can idle a crew for days.

Add it up honestly and December offers maybe 14 to 16 genuinely productive days. Schedule it as if it has 21 and you have built a two-week slip into the plan before the first snowflake falls.

How we schedule framing through the holidays

Front-load material now

Everything we need through mid-January gets ordered this week and delivered by the third week of December. Material sitting on site through the holidays costs a little staging space. Material stuck at a shuttered truss plant costs a full crew standing around on January 2.

Pick one pre-Christmas milestone and protect it

On a multi-family job that milestone is usually a building dried in, a deck complete, or a floor topped out — something that leaves the project weather-tight and restart-ready. Everything in the next three weeks bends toward that milestone, and the nice-to-haves wait. Our multi-family framing schedule breakdown shows how we sequence those phases in a normal month; a holiday month just compresses the same logic.

Lock crew calendars before December

We collect holiday plans from every crew lead in November, so by now we know exactly who is on site which days, and our GCs get that calendar in writing. Surprise absences — not the holidays themselves — are what actually kill December production.

Book inspections before the dead week

Anything that needs an inspector's signature should be scheduled to pass before December 22. A failed inspection on the 23rd does not get re-inspected until January, and now insulation and drywall are stacked up behind it while the building sits.

Use the week between the holidays deliberately

December 26 through 31 is a bad week for production framing and a good week for a small crew doing backframing, blocking, cleanup, and layout for the January push. We staff it that way on purpose instead of pretending a full crew will show up.

Cold is manageable — chaos is not

We frame all winter across Minnesota, North Dakota, Wisconsin, and Montana, and cold-weather production is a solved problem: material staged close, shorter exposed tasks, generous fastener and glue adjustments, and crews that have done it for years. What is not solvable after the fact is a December that was never planned. If you are a GC lining up trades for the winter stretch, our page for general contractors covers how we communicate schedule, manpower, and two-week look-aheads — the holidays are exactly when that communication earns its keep.

Trying to keep a project moving through the holidays, or locking in a framing crew for the new year? Get in touch and we will build a schedule that survives December.
